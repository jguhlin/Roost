Here’s the current three-figure arc we’ve converged on.

### Figure 1 — What Roost is
**Purpose:** establish the resource, its scale, and what makes it different.

Core story:
**fragmented literature + surveillance + genomes → integrated host-side knowledgebase**

Current structure:
- **Literature:** 1,366 papers from 22 sources → literature evidence via agents + QA
- **Surveillance:** 44,842 detections across 106 countries / 714 detected species → species × occurrence
- **Genomes:** 3,894 assemblies catalogued, 2,358 screened → presence + orthology
- These converge into **ROOST**, integrating species, genes, mechanisms, evidence, occurrence and genomics.
- Headline scale around Roost:
  - **2,048 species indexed**
  - **4,277 host genes tracked**
  - likely **997 genes studied in ≥2 papers** rather than the orthogroup count
  - **2.95M gene–genome assessments**

Then a concrete example:
- **ANP32A**
- avian +33 aa insertion vs mammalian absence
- links sequence variation → influenza polymerase compatibility / host range → comparative genomics → source-linked evidence
- followed directly by a **real screenshot of the ANP32 explorer**, with callouts for comparative genomics, mechanism and source evidence.

**Take-home:** Roost does not just collect records; it connects literature, biology, genomes and surveillance into a traceable, explorable host-genetics resource.

### Figure 2 — What becomes visible when the literature is synthesized
**Purpose:** show that Roost produces biological synthesis, not just retrieval.

The likely opening result is the **extreme long tail in host-gene evidence**:
- 3,515 host genes directly studied
- **2,518 (71.6%) appear in only one paper**
- 615 in 2–3 papers
- 252 in 4–9
- 80 in 10–19
- 50 in 20+
- therefore only **997 genes are studied in ≥2 papers**

That gives the central result:
> the literature is large, but evidence is highly fragmented and uneven.

Then move from genes to mechanisms/pathways.

The figure would likely contain:
1. **Evidence-depth distribution** — the long tail.
2. **Global molecular/pathway landscape** — what major biological processes emerge across the literature.
3. **Canonical pathway/mechanism** — a positive-control example showing Roost recovers well-established H5N1 host biology.
4. **Under-recognized but strongly supported pathway/mechanism** — the value-add: multiple papers/genes independently converge on something less obvious when evidence is integrated.

If space is tight, the canonical pathway can be visible within the global landscape and the detailed panel can go to the under-recognized mechanism.

**Take-home:** integrating 1,300+ papers reveals both where evidence is genuinely deep and where dispersed studies converge on host mechanisms that are difficult to see from individual publications.

### Figure 3 — Where the knowledge gaps are
**Purpose:** turn the database into an actionable conservation/research result.

This is the **blind-spot figure**.

Core comparison:
**what species/genomes are available** versus **what species have actually received host-genetics research attention**.

Likely components:
- all or most indexed avian species, ideally phylogenetically/taxonomically ordered;
- genome availability / genomic analysis status;
- amount of H5N1 host-genetics literature;
- perhaps documented infection status;
- possibly conservation status if useful and defensible.

The key category is:

> **genome-rich, knowledge-poor species**

These are species where genomic data already exist but there is little or no H5N1 host-genetic research.

Potential summaries:
- research effort is concentrated in a small number of model/agricultural/commonly studied species;
- large parts of avian diversity remain host-genetically understudied;
- many of those gaps are **immediately tractable**, because the genomes already exist;
- especially compelling subsets could be species with documented H5N1 occurrence and an available genome but minimal host-genetic work.

Importantly, this is **not** a susceptibility score. The claim is about research opportunity and information gaps, not predicting which species will die.

**Take-home:** host-genetic knowledge lags far behind available genomic resources, and Roost identifies where existing data could be used immediately to expand host-focused H5N1 research.

So the overall three-figure narrative is very clean:

**Fig. 1 — We built the missing host-side infrastructure.**  
**Fig. 2 — Integrating the evidence changes what we can see biologically.**  
**Fig. 3 — Integrating species, literature and genomes shows where the field should look next.**

That feels like a coherent paper rather than “three nice things the database can do.”
